**Imagine you're in a room.**

How many people do you think you'd need before **two people are likely to share the same birthday?**

A hundred?
Fifty?
Maybe even more?

Surprisingly, you only need **23 people**.

With just 23 people in a room, there's already about a **50% chance** that at least two people share the same birthday.

Sounds impossible, right?

This surprising result is called the **Birthday Paradox**.

So why does this happen?

Let's uncover the math behind the Birthday Paradox.

Now let's generalize the problem.

Suppose there are **(n)** people in a room.

Our question is:

**What's the probability that at least two people share the same birthday?**

To keep things simple, let's assume a year has **365 days** and ignore leap years.

Now, if **(n > 365)**, the answer is obvious.

By the **Pigeonhole Principle**, at least two people **must** share the same birthday.

The interesting case is when **(n < 365)**.

At first glance, it seems we should calculate the probability that **some pair** has the same birthday.

But that's surprisingly difficult.

Instead, we'll solve the opposite problem.

We'll first calculate the probability that **everyone has a different birthday**.

Once we know that, finding the probability that **at least one pair shares a birthday** is easy.

It's simply:

[
P(\text{at least one match}) = 1 - P(\text{no matches})
]

So let's begin by finding the probability that no two people share the same birthday.
